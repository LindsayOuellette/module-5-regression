    Regression

    This week we're building on correlation. A lot will feel similar — but with one big difference.

    The difference

    Correlation measures the strength of a relationship. Regression measures causation — a change in X causes a change in Y.

    In one sentence

    Regression lets us predict the value of one variable based on another.

    Quick recap

    Correlation: how strong is the relationship?

    Last week we measured how closely two variables are related using r, the correlation coefficient. Remember the range:

    The closer the dots are to forming a line, the stronger the correlation. But remember: correlation does not mean causation.

    Key distinction

    Correlation ≠ Causation

    Just because two variables are related doesn't mean one causes the other.

    Example

    Wearing shorts and ice cream sales are highly correlated.

    But wearing shorts doesn't cause you to buy ice cream. And buying ice cream doesn't cause you to wear shorts.

    A third variable — warm weather — is behind both.

    Correlation says:

    "These two things tend to go together."

    We're just describing how related they are.

    If you change one variable, it does not necessarily mean a change in the other.

    Where regression comes in

    This is where regression comes in. Regression lets us talk about causation — a change in X will cause a change in Y.

    The big idea

    Regression = Prediction

    In regression, we work with independent and dependent variables — and we're saying that a change in the IV causes a change in the DV.

    Independent variable (IV)

    Always on the X axis

    The predictor — the thing we think causes the change

    Dependent variable (DV)

    Always on the Y axis

    The outcome — the thing that changes as a result

    Think of it this way

    Think of it like a fortune teller: if I know your value of X, I can predict your value of Y.

    Building blocks

    Slope: the rate of change

    The regression line shows us the rate of change. You might remember "rise over run" from algebra — same idea.

    The slope tells us

    When X increases by one unit, Y changes by the slope amount.

    Δ (delta) = rate of change. Slope = ΔY / ΔX

    Real example

    Parental SES → Student test scores

    Let's say our IV is parental socioeconomic status and our DV is student test scores. The slope (rate of change) is 5 test points.

    Interpretation

    As parental SES increases by one unit, student test scores increase by 5 points.

    Because this is a positive slope, we know it's a positive relationship: as X goes up, Y goes up.

    Instructor note

    This is just like correlation — a positive slope means a positive direction, a negative slope means negative. The difference is we're now saying one causes the other.

    Another way to think about it

    Comparing two students

    Interpretation

    If we compare two students whose parents differ in SES by $5,000, the student whose parent makes $5,000 more will have higher test scores by 5 points.

    Student A's parents make $10,000. Student B's parents make $15,000. That's a $5,000 difference → 5-point difference in test scores.

    Building blocks

    The intercept

    The intercept is the predicted value of Y when X equals zero — the starting point of the regression line.

    In our example

    When parental SES is zero (parents make no money), student test scores are predicted to be 10 points.

    Good news: when we interpret and write up regression results, we don't really talk about the intercept. But you should know what it is.

    Where the name comes from

    Regression to the mean

    The word "regress" means to return to a former or less developed state. Francis Galton, who invented regression, called it "regression to mediocrity."

    The pattern

    Extreme values tend to move back toward the average over time.

    The top always comes down. The bottom always comes up.

    Examples

    Baseball: The rookie of the year almost always does worse in their second year.

    Schools: The bottom-performing school almost always does better the next year.

    The regression line is essentially the prediction line — predicting that things will trend back toward the middle.

    The math

    The regression equation

    You don't need to do calculations, but you should recognize the equation and know what each piece means.

    Y = b₀ + b₁X₁

    Y

    The predicted value of the dependent variable

    b₀ (beta zero)

    The intercept — the predicted Y when X = 0

    b₁ (beta one)

    The slope — the rate of change

    X₁

    The value of the independent variable for a given case

    The "b" values are often called beta (β). You'll see both lowercase β (standardized) and uppercase B (unstandardized) in output. Same idea — just different scales.

    Putting it together

    The regression line = the prediction line

    The regression line is drawn through all the data points to make the best predictions. The distance between each dot and the line is the residual (error) — how far off the prediction was.

    Interactive: Residuals ExplorerDrag dots toward or away from the line
    Average residual: —

    Points close to the line

    Small residuals → good predictions → strong effect of IV on DV

    Points far from the line

    Large residuals → poor predictions → weak effect of IV on DV

    The regression line minimizes the total squared residuals — it's the line that makes the least total error across all data points.

    Effect size

    Variance in regression: R²

    In regression, we use R² (R-squared) to describe the effect size — how useful is our predictor?

    What R² tells us

    The percentage of variance in the DV that is explained by the IV.

    Example: R² = 0.40 means the IV explains 40% of the changes in the DV.

    Benchmarks

    .01 – .09 → Small effect

    .09 – .25 → Medium effect

    .25+ → Large effect

    Similar to correlation coefficient benchmarks.

    How to say it

    "The IV accounts for [R² × 100]% of the variance in the DV."

    You only need a few numbers from this wall of text: the slope, the R², the F-statistic with its degrees of freedom, and the p-value. Those go into your APA write-up.

    Writing it up

    APA reporting for regression

    Values to report

    R², F value, degrees of freedom (numerator, denominator), p value, and β for each predictor.

    Example write-up

    A simple linear regression was used to test if years since PhD significantly predicted professor salary. The results indicated that years since PhD explained 17.6% of the variance in salary (R² = .18, F(1, 395) = 84.23, p < .001). Years since PhD significantly predicted salary (β = 985, p < .001).

    Notice the pattern: name the test → report R² and what it means → report F with degrees of freedom and p → report β for the predictor with its p.

    Recap

    Module 5: what to remember

    1. Regression ≠ correlation

    Correlation describes relationships. Regression describes causation and prediction: a change in the IV causes a change in the DV.

    2. The slope

    For every 1-unit increase in X, Y changes by the slope. It's the rate of change.

    3. The intercept

    The predicted value of Y when X = 0. You usually don't need to interpret it.

    4. R² = effect size

    The percentage of variance in the DV explained by the IV. Higher = stronger effect.

    5. The equation

    Y = b₀ + b₁X₁

    6. APA format

    Report R², F(df₁, df₂), p, and β for each predictor.

    For your final paper: if you have two variables, a correlation or regression will likely be the easiest option.
